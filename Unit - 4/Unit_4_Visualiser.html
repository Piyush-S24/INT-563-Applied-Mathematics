1Graph basics

A graph is a set of dots joined by lines. Every question in this unit is a question about those dots and lines.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Everything else in this unit is defined in terms of vertices, edges and degree. Get these three right and Dijkstra, colouring and max-flow are all just rules about them; get them wrong and nothing later can be stated precisely.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Just draw the picture and look at it” works for six vertices and collapses at sixty. A drawing also has no notion of the same graph drawn differently — two pictures that look nothing alike can be identical graphs. The degree sequence and the adjacency matrix are what let you compare them.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You cannot check your own answer. The handshaking lemma is the cheapest correctness test in the unit — if your degrees do not sum to twice the edge count, you have miscounted, and you find out in ten seconds instead of at the end of the question.

Degree of a vertex
deg(v) = number of edges touching v
A self-loop counts twice — both of its ends touch the same vertex.
Handshaking lemma
 ∑v ∈ V deg(v) = 2|E|
Handshaking lemma. Every edge has two ends, so adding up all the degrees counts every edge exactly twice.
Consequence
The number of odd-degree vertices is always even. You can never build a graph with exactly three odd vertices — try it and the handshaking sum comes out odd.
Try it — click any edge to remove or restore it, and watch the degrees change

Adjacency matrix and adjacency list

Two ways to store the same graph. The matrix answers “is u joined to v?” instantly; the list is far smaller when the graph is sparse.

Adjacency matrix

Row sums are the degrees. Space is always n².

Adjacency list

Space is O(n + m) — the usual choice.

Worked example — verifying the handshaking lemma
The graph in the panel above: V = {A, B, C, D, E} with 7 edges — AB, AD, BC, BD, BE, CE, DE.
  1. Count the edges touching each vertexdeg(A) = 2, deg(B) = 4, deg(C) = 2, deg(D) = 3, deg(E) = 3
  2. Add the degrees up2 + 4 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 14
  3. Compare with twice the edge count2|E| = 2 × 7 = 14  ✓
  4. Now count the odd-degree verticesD and E have odd degree — 2 of them, an even number, exactly as the lemma forces
Both checks pass. Had your degree sum come to 13, you would know at once that an edge was miscounted — an odd total is impossible in any graph. Click an edge off in the panel above and watch the sum drop by exactly 2.

2Graph types

The name of a graph tells you exactly what you may assume about it.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

The type of a graph is a licence to assume things. Knowing a graph is a tree tells you instantly that it has n − 1 edges, no cycles, and exactly one path between any two vertices — three facts you would otherwise have to prove.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not treat every graph as a general graph?” You can, and you will do far more work than necessary. Shortest path on a tree is a single walk; on a general weighted graph it needs Dijkstra. Two-colouring a bipartite graph is one BFS; deciding χ in general is NP-hard. The structure is the shortcut.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You reach for the heavy algorithm when the light one would do, or — worse — you assume a property the graph does not have. Assume a graph is bipartite when it contains a triangle and your two-colouring will simply fail, with no clear reason why.

3Graph traversing algorithms

Both visit every reachable vertex exactly once. The only difference is the data structure holding the frontier — and that single choice changes everything.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Before you can find anything in a graph you need a way to visit it systematically, without missing a vertex and without going round a cycle forever. BFS and DFS are the two ways, and almost every graph algorithm is one of them with extra bookkeeping.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why have two, if both visit everything?” Because the order is what you actually use. BFS reaches every vertex by the fewest hops, so it solves unweighted shortest path for free; DFS goes deep first, which is what cycle detection, topological sort and connected components need. Swapping the queue for a stack changes the whole character of the search.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Forget the visited set and the traversal never terminates on a cyclic graph — it walks round the cycle indefinitely. This is the single most common bug in first implementations, and it is why the mark scheme asks you to show the visited set.

BFS — breadth first

Frontier is a queue (first in, first out). Explores in rings of increasing distance, so on an unweighted graph it finds the shortest path.

DFS — depth first

Frontier is a stack (last in, first out). Dives as deep as it can before backing up. Gives discovery/finish times and a topological order on a DAG.

unvisitedin frontiervisitedcurrent
Why BFS gives shortest paths
A queue hands vertices back in the order they were found, so every vertex at distance k leaves the queue before any vertex at distance k + 1. The first time you reach a vertex is therefore along a shortest route.
Worked example — BFS by hand, with the queue written out at every step
The 6-vertex graph in the panel above. Adjacency: A → B, C  |  B → A, D  |  C → A, E  |  D → B, F  |  E → C, F  |  F → D, E. Start at A.
  1. Startqueue = [A]   visited = {A}
  2. Pop A — push its unvisited neighbours B and Cqueue = [B, C]   visited = {A, B, C}
  3. Pop B — A is already visited, so only D is addedqueue = [C, D]   visited = {A, B, C, D}
  4. Pop C — adds Equeue = [D, E]   visited = {A, B, C, D, E}
  5. Pop D — adds Fqueue = [E, F]   visited = all six
  6. Pop E, then Fevery neighbour is already visited — the queue empties and BFS stops
BFS order: A → B → C → D → E → F. Because the frontier is a queue, this is also the order of increasing hop-distance from A: A at 0, B and C at 1, D and E at 2, F at 3. Switch the selector to DFS and the same graph gives a completely different order.

4Graph searching algorithms

Now the edges carry weights, and “shortest” means smallest total weight, not fewest hops.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Once edges carry weights, “fewest hops” and “cheapest” stop being the same thing. A three-hop route down a motorway beats a one-hop route down a lane. Dijkstra finds the cheapest route rather than the shortest-looking one.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just use BFS?” BFS counts hops and ignores weights entirely, so it will happily return a 1-edge path of cost 40 over a 3-edge path of cost 9. “Why not try every path?” Because the number of paths grows factorially — fine for six vertices, hopeless for six hundred.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Use Dijkstra on a graph with a negative edge and it returns a confidently wrong answer. Dijkstra finalises a vertex the moment it is cheapest on the frontier, assuming no later path can improve it — which a negative edge breaks. That is exactly the case Bellman–Ford exists for.

Dijkstra’s shortest path

The relaxation step
if  d[u] + w(u, v) < d[v]  then  d[v] ← d[u] + w(u, v)
This single operation is called relaxation, and it is the only thing Dijkstra does. Settle the nearest unsettled vertex, relax its edges, repeat.
step 0 of 0
Why Dijkstra needs non-negative weights
It commits to a vertex the instant that vertex is nearest, and never looks at it again. A negative edge discovered later could have made a settled vertex cheaper, so the answer would be wrong. Use Bellman–Ford instead, which relaxes every edge n − 1 times and also detects negative cycles.

Minimum spanning tree — Kruskal

A different goal: connect every vertex as cheaply as possible, with no cycles. An MST on n vertices always has exactly n − 1 edges.

Kruskal vs Prim
Kruskal sorts all edges and grows a forest; Prim grows one tree outward from a start vertex. When every weight is distinct the MST is unique, so both return exactly the same tree.
Worked example — one relaxation step, written out in full
The weighted graph in the panel above. Edge weights include A–B = 4, A–C = 2 and B–C = 1. We are part-way through Dijkstra from A: C has just been finalised with d[C] = 2, and B currently holds d[B] = 4 from the direct edge.
  1. The relaxation ruleif d[u] + w(u, v) < d[v], then d[v] → d[u] + w(u, v)
  2. Substitute the numbers for the edge C–Bd[C] + w(C, B) = 2 + 1 = 3
  3. Compare with the estimate B already holds3 < 4  →  going via C is cheaper than going direct
  4. Update both the distance and the predecessord[B] = 3, and B's predecessor becomes C instead of A
B was reachable in one hop for cost 4, but two hops through C cost only 3. Relaxation is nothing more than this one comparison, repeated until no estimate can improve — which is why the final answer to F is 13 via A → C → E → F and not the 1-hop-looking route.

5Graph colouring problem

Colour the vertices so that no edge joins two of the same colour. The fewest colours needed is the chromatic number χ(G).

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

A surprising number of real scheduling problems are this one problem in disguise. Anything of the form these two things clash, so they cannot share a slot is graph colouring — exam timetables, radio frequencies, registers in a compiler.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just give everything its own slot?” That always works and is always wasteful — it is a 30-day exam timetable when 3 days would do. The whole value is in using the fewest colours. “Why not find the true minimum?” Because deciding χ is NP-hard, so for any real instance you use a greedy heuristic and accept a good answer.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You assume greedy gives the minimum. Welsh–Powell is a heuristic: it guarantees at most Δ + 1 colours, not the fewest. Quoting its output as χ is a real mark-losing error unless you can also show a lower bound — such as a clique that forces that many colours.

Upper bound on the chromatic number
χ(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 1
The greedy bound. A vertex has at most Δ neighbours, so among Δ + 1 colours one is always still free when you reach it.

Worked example — Welsh–Powell, and then proving the answer is optimal
The 6-vertex graph in the panel above: edges AB, AC, BC, CD, CE, EF, DF. Degrees: C=4, and A, B, D, E, F all =2.
  1. Sort the vertices by degree, highest firstC(4), A(2), B(2), D(2), E(2), F(2)
  2. Colour 1 — start with C, then take anything not adjacent to what is already in this colourC takes colour 1. A, B, D and E are all adjacent to C, so they are skipped. F is not adjacent to C → F joins colour 1.  colour 1 = {C, F}
  3. Colour 2 — highest remaining is AA takes colour 2. B is adjacent to A, skip. D is not adjacent to A → joins. E is adjacent to neither A nor D → joins.  colour 2 = {A, D, E}
  4. Colour 3 — only B is leftcolour 3 = {B}, so 3 colours were used
  5. Check the upper boundχ ≤ Δ + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5 — we did better, at 3
  6. Now the lower bound, which is what proves optimalityA, B and C are mutually adjacent (AB, AC and BC are all edges), so they form a triangle and need three different colours between them → χ ≥ 3
Upper bound 3 from the colouring, lower bound 3 from the triangle, so χ(G) = 3 exactly. Welsh–Powell happens to hit the optimum here — but it is the triangle, not the algorithm, that entitles you to say so.

6Max-flow min-cut algorithm

How much can you push from source s to sink t without exceeding any pipe’s capacity?

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Capacity problems are everywhere — bandwidth, pipelines, roads, assigning staff to shifts. Max-flow answers “how much can get through?” and, through the min-cut, also tells you which specific links are the bottleneck, which is the part you can act on.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just add up the capacities leaving the source?” Because a narrow pipe further downstream caps everything behind it — the limit is rarely at the source. “Why not push greedily down the fattest path?” Greedy can commit flow to an edge that a better routing needed, and get stuck below the maximum. The residual graph exists precisely so flow can be undone.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You report a flow that is not maximal and cannot tell. Without computing the cut you have no certificate — finding a cut whose capacity equals your flow is the proof that you are finished, and it is the only way to be sure.

The theorem
max flow = min cut capacity
Ford–Fulkerson: while an augmenting path still exists in the residual graph, push its bottleneck along it. When no path remains, the flow is maximum — and equals the capacity of the smallest cut.
Reading the minimum cut off the final picture
When no augmenting path remains, mark every vertex still reachable from s. The edges leaving that set form the minimum cut, and their capacities add up to exactly the maximum flow.
Worked example — using the cut to prove a flow is maximal
A flow of value 13 has been found. The two edges leaving s have capacities 8 and 5.
  1. Pick the cutput only s on the source side: S = {s}, T = everything else
  2. Add the capacities of edges crossing forwards8 + 5 = 13
  3. Apply the theoremevery flow ≤ every cut, so max flow ≤ 13
  4. Compare with the flow we haveour flow is 13, which already meets that ceiling
Flow 13 = cut 13, so the flow is maximal and this cut is minimal. Both edges out of s are saturated — widening anything else in the network would change nothing.
