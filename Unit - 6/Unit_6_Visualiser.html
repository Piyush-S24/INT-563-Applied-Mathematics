1Greedy approach

Take the locally best option at every step and never reconsider. Fast, simple, and correct only when the problem has the right structure.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Greedy is the cheapest thing that can possibly work: one pass, no table, no recursion, and an answer that is often optimal. When the problem has the right structure it is simply the correct algorithm — and it is the first thing worth trying.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not use greedy everywhere, since it is fastest?” Because for most problems it is wrong, and wrong silently. It never revisits a choice, so a decision that looked best early can make the rest of the problem unsolvable or merely poor — and the output still looks like a perfectly reasonable answer.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You get a plausible, confidently wrong answer with no warning. On 0/1 knapsack with capacity 10 and items (6, ₹30), (5, ₹24), (5, ₹24), greedy by density takes the first item and finishes on ₹30; the optimum is ₹48. Nothing in the output signals the 38% shortfall.

When greedy works

Greedy-choice property — a globally optimal solution can be reached by making locally optimal choices.
Optimal substructure — the optimal solution contains optimal solutions to its subproblems.

When greedy fails

0/1 knapsack. Capacity 10 with items (w=6, p=30), (5, 24), (5, 24). Greedy by density picks item 1 (5.0) then has 4 units left and nothing fits → profit 30. The optimum takes items 2 and 3 → profit 48.

Activity selection — sort by finish time

Given activities with start and finish times, pick the largest set that do not overlap. The greedy rule is always take the activity that finishes earliest among those still compatible — it leaves the most room for everything after it.

step 0 of 0
Why earliest-finish is the right rule
Suppose an optimal solution starts with some activity a. Swap it for the earliest-finishing activity a1. Since finish(a1) ≤ finish(a), nothing that followed a can now clash, so the swapped set is still optimal and just as large. Repeat and you have built the greedy solution.
Worked example — fractional knapsack, where greedy is provably optimal
Capacity W = 50. Items: A (w=10, p=60), B (20, 100), C (30, 120). Items may be cut, which is the property that makes greedy correct here.
  1. Compute profit per unit weight for each itemA: 6010 = 6.0  ·  B: 10020 = 5.0  ·  C: 12030 = 4.0
  2. Sort by density, highest firstA (6.0), then B (5.0), then C (4.0)
  3. Take A whole — it fitsweight used 10, profit +60, remaining capacity 50 − 10 = 40
  4. Take B whole — it also fitsweight used 20, profit +100, remaining capacity 40 − 20 = 20
  5. Only 20 of C's 30 units fit, so take the fraction2030 = 23 of C  →  profit + 23 × 120 = 80
  6. Total60 + 100 + 80 = 240
Maximum profit 240. Because items can be divided, there is never a reason to pass over a denser item for a less dense one — which is exactly the greedy-choice property, and exactly what 0/1 knapsack lacks.

2Dynamic programming

Solve every subproblem once, store the answer, and reuse it. Where greedy guesses, DP checks both options and keeps the better one.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Dynamic programming is what you use when greedy's single pass is not enough but brute force is too slow. It checks both options at every decision — take it or skip it — and the only reason that stays affordable is that the same subproblems recur, so each is solved once and reused.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not plain recursion?” Plain recursion on 0/1 knapsack recomputes the same subproblem exponentially many times; adding a cache is the whole difference between 2n and nW. “Why not greedy, it is faster?” Greedy is wrong here — see the previous section's ₹30 versus ₹48.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You apply DP to a problem without optimal substructure and the table fills with values that do not compose. DP is only valid when an optimal solution genuinely contains optimal solutions to its subproblems; longest simple path in a graph fails this, and no amount of table-filling will fix it.

Memoisation (top-down)

Write the natural recursion, then cache each result. Only the subproblems you actually need get computed.

Tabulation (bottom-up)

Fill a table from the smallest subproblem upward. No recursion overhead and the table itself is the proof of correctness.

Greedy vs DP on the same problem

Worked example — filling one cell of the 0/1 knapsack table
Items (w, p): 1=(2,3), 2=(3,4), 3=(4,5), 4=(5,6); capacity 5. Rows 1 and 2 are already filled; compute V[3][5] — the best profit using only items 1–3 with capacity 5.
  1. Write the recurrenceV[i][w] = max [V[i−1][w],  pi + V[i−1][w − wi]]
  2. Option A — skip item 3take the value directly above: V[2][5] = 7
  3. Option B — take item 3 (weight 4, profit 5)it fits in 5, so collect 5 and fill the remaining 5 − 4 = 1 from the row above: 5 + V[2][1] = 5 + 0 = 5
  4. Take the better of the twomax(7, 5) = 7, so V[3][5] = 7 — item 3 is not taken
  5. Finish the table and read the cornerV[4][5] = 7
  6. Trace back to recover which itemsa cell differing from the one above means that item was taken → items 1 and 2, weight 2+3 = 5, profit 3+4 = 7  ✓
Best profit 7, from items 1 and 2. Greedy by density would have taken item 1 (1.50) then item 2 (1.33) and reached the same answer here — but it has no way of knowing it was right, and on the capacity-10 example above it is not.

3Divide and conquer

Split the problem into independent pieces, solve each recursively, then combine. The running time falls straight out of the Master theorem.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Divide and conquer turns one large problem into several small independent ones. Because the pieces do not interact, they can be solved in any order — or in parallel — and the total cost drops straight out of the Master theorem.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not solve it directly in one pass?” For some problems you can, and should. D&C pays off when combining two solved halves is cheaper than solving the whole: merging two sorted halves costs n, while sorting the whole thing from scratch costs far more. “Why not divide into more pieces?” Sometimes you should — that is exactly what Karatsuba and Strassen do differently.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You use it where the subproblems overlap. D&C assumes the pieces are independent; when they are not, you recompute the same work repeatedly — naive recursive Fibonacci is the classic case, exponential where DP makes it linear.

The general shape
T(n) = divide + a T(nb) + combine
Three costs. For merge sort dividing is free, a = b = 2, and combining (the merge) costs n.

Worked example — Karatsuba — three multiplications instead of four
Multiplying two 2-digit numbers, 12 × 34, by splitting each in half. Write a = 12 = 1·10 + 2 and b = 34 = 3·10 + 4, so a1=1, a0=2, b1=3, b0=4.
  1. The schoolbook method needs four productsa1b1, a1b0, a0b1, a0b0  →  T(n) = 4T(n/2) + n = θ(n2)
  2. Karatsuba computes only threez2 = a1b1 = 1×3 = 3;  z0 = a0b0 = 2×4 = 8
  3. The third product is of the sums(a1+a0)(b1+b0) = (1+2)(3+4) = 3×7 = 21
  4. Recover the middle term by subtraction, not multiplicationz1 = 21 − z2 − z0 = 21 − 3 − 8 = 10
  5. Reassemblez2·100 + z1·10 + z0 = 300 + 100 + 8 = 408
  6. Check12 × 34 = 408  ✓
  7. What the saving costs asymptoticallyT(n) = 3T(n/2) + n → θ(nlog2 3) = θ(n1.585)
Three multiplications and two subtractions replaced four multiplications. On 2-digit numbers that is no saving at all — but applied recursively to thousand-digit numbers it drops the exponent from 2 to 1.585, which is the whole point.

4Back tracking

Build a candidate one decision at a time and abandon a partial candidate the moment it cannot possibly succeed. A depth-first search of the decision tree with pruning.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Backtracking is exhaustive search that gives up early. The moment a partial candidate breaks a constraint, every completion of it is discarded at once — so one test can eliminate an entire subtree rather than one leaf.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not generate every candidate and filter at the end?” Because that builds bd complete candidates before rejecting almost all of them. Backtracking rejects a 4-queens placement after two queens, killing all completions of it unseen. “Why not greedy?” Greedy cannot undo, and these problems require undoing — that is what the “back” means.

What goes wrong if you skip it

You forget to un-choose on the way back up. The partial solution is then polluted by a decision that was supposed to have been withdrawn, and the search explores nonsense — typically returning no solution at all for a problem that has many.

Choose

Make the next decision — place a queen, pick a number, colour a vertex.

Check

Test the partial solution against the constraints. If it already fails, stop here.

Recurse / un-choose

If it is still viable, go deeper. On return, undo the choice and try the next option.

4-Queens — every placement, every dead end

No two queens may share a row, column or diagonal. Watch how the search abandons whole branches as soon as a column becomes impossible.

Why pruning matters
nodes visited ≪ bd
A plain exhaustive search explores bd leaves. Pruning cuts entire subtrees, so backtracking visits far fewer nodes — although the worst case is still exponential.
Backtracking vs branch and bound
Backtracking prunes on feasibility: a partial solution that breaks a constraint is dropped. Branch and bound also prunes on cost: it computes a bound on the best achievable value down a branch and drops the branch if that bound cannot beat the best solution found so far.
Worked example — 4-Queens — counting what pruning saves
Place 4 queens on a 4×4 board, one per row, no two sharing a column or diagonal.
  1. Size of the raw search space, one queen per row44 = 256 placements to test if you generate them all first
  2. With the column constraint built in4! = 24 permutations
  3. What backtracking actually does — the first dead endplace (r0,c0), then (r1,c2); now every column in row 2 is attacked, so the search abandons this branch after 3 placements
  4. How much that one rejection savedthat branch contained 4 × 4 = 16 complete placements, none of which was ever built
  5. Continue until the first solutionbacktrack to row 1, try c3, descend again  →  columns (1, 3, 0, 2)
  6. Verify itthe columns 1, 3, 0, 2 are all distinct  ✓  and for every pair of queens |Δcol| ≠ |Δrow|, so no two share a diagonal  ✓
Solution (1, 3, 0, 2); its mirror (2, 0, 3, 1) is the only other one. The step-through panel above visits roughly 30 nodes instead of 256 — and on an 8×8 board that ratio is what makes the problem solvable at all.

5Genetic algorithm

Keep a population of candidate solutions, score them, breed the good ones, mutate a little, repeat. A search method for problems where you can score a solution but cannot differentiate anything.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

Some problems give you no gradient to follow and no structure to exploit — you can score a candidate solution but you cannot differentiate the scoring function or reason about which direction improves it. A genetic algorithm needs nothing but that score, which is why it applies where almost nothing else does.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not just try random solutions?” Random search throws away everything it learns. A GA keeps the good candidates and recombines them, so partial solutions accumulate across generations. “Why not hill-climb?” Hill-climbing gets stuck in the first local optimum it finds; a population explores several regions at once.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Without elitism the best solution found can be lost to crossover and never recovered — the population's best score goes down between generations. And with mutation set too high the search degenerates into the random search it was supposed to improve on.

The two ways it goes wrong
Premature convergence — one good chromosome takes over the population and the search stops exploring. Fix it with a larger population, tournament selection or a higher mutation rate. No convergence — mutation so high that the search is random. Typical settings: population 50–200, crossover rate 0.6–0.9, mutation rate 0.001–0.05.
Worked example — one complete generation, maximising f(x) = x2
Chromosomes are 5-bit integers, so x runs 0–31 and the best possible fitness is 312 = 961. Initial population: 01101, 11000, 01000, 10011.

6Nature inspired algorithms

A family of metaheuristics that copy a mechanism from nature. None guarantees the optimum; all of them trade that guarantee for being able to search a space no exact method can handle.

Why this, and why not something else
Why we do this

These methods exist for the gap between “small enough to solve exactly” and “structured enough for a greedy or DP algorithm”. In that gap you cannot afford the exact answer and have no shortcut — so you trade the guarantee for a good answer in reasonable time.

Why not just do the simpler thing?

“Why not use an exact method?” Always do, if the instance is small enough — a guarantee beats a good guess. “Why not hill-climb?” Because it stops at the first local optimum. Simulated annealing's whole trick is deliberately accepting worse solutions early, which is the one thing hill-climbing refuses to do and the only way out of a local basin.

What goes wrong if you skip it

Cool too fast and it freezes into the first basin it meets — you have paid for annealing and received hill-climbing. Cool too slowly and it never settles. The cooling schedule is the whole algorithm, and α ≈ 0.95 is a starting point, not an answer.

Simulated annealing

Copies the cooling of metal. Accepts a worse solution with probability exp(−ΔE / T) and lowers T over time — bold early, fussy later.

Ant colony optimisation

Ants lay pheromone on the paths they take; a shorter path is reinforced faster because the round trip finishes sooner. Pheromone also evaporates, so poor paths fade. Built for routing and the travelling salesman problem.

Particle swarm optimisation

Each particle remembers its own best position and is pulled toward the swarm's best. Velocity is a blend of inertia, personal best and global best.

Artificial bee colony

Employed bees exploit known food sources, onlookers choose sources by quality, and scouts abandon exhausted sources to explore at random.

Metropolis acceptance criterion
P(accept) = exp(−ΔET),   Tk+1 = αTk,  α ≈ 0.95
Simulated annealing. When T is large the exponent is near 0 so almost any move is accepted and the search roams freely. As T falls the same uphill move becomes very unlikely, and the search settles into a minimum.

Particle swarm update. w is inertia, pi the particle's own best, g the swarm's best, and r1, r2 are random numbers in [0, 1] that keep the swarm from collapsing onto one line.
How to choose between them
Exact method (DP, branch and bound) if the instance is small enough — it gives a guarantee. Greedy if the problem has the greedy-choice property — it is the fastest correct answer. A metaheuristic only when the space is too large for an exact method and you can live with a good answer rather than the best one.
Worked example — the Metropolis criterion at three temperatures
A proposed move makes things worse by ΔE = 0.50. Whether it is accepted depends entirely on the current temperature: P = exp(−ΔE / T).
  1. Early in the run, T = 2.00P = exp(−0.50 / 2.00) = exp(−0.25) = 0.779 → accepted about 78% of the time — the search roams freely
  2. Mid-run, T = 0.50P = exp(−0.50 / 0.50) = exp(−1) = 0.368 → still escapes basins, but is becoming choosy
  3. Late, T = 0.10P = exp(−0.50 / 0.10) = exp(−5) = 0.0067 → essentially refused; only downhill moves get through
  4. Note what never changesΔE is 0.50 in all three lines — the same move is welcomed, tolerated, then rejected purely because T fell
  5. And a downhill move at any temperatureΔE ≤ 0 → P = 1, always accepted
The temperature alone turns an explorer into an optimiser. That is why the cooling schedule Tk+1 = αTk is the algorithm — everything else is bookkeeping.
