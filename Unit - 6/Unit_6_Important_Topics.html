1Greedy approach: fractional knapsack, activity selection, Huffman  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Builds a solution step by step by always taking the choice that looks best right now.

The concept

Greedy works when the problem has the greedy-choice property and optimal substructure. Fractional knapsack: highest value/weight first. Activity selection: earliest finish first. Huffman: merge the two smallest frequencies.

Where it is used

Scheduling, compression (Huffman codes in ZIP and JPEG), MST and Dijkstra.

Knapsack
fractional knapsack: sort by vi/wi, take whole items, then a fraction of the next
Huffman
Huffman: repeatedly merge the two smallest weights; total cost = sum of all merged weights = Σ freq × code length
Worked example — fractional knapsack and activity selection
(i) Capacity 50; items (value, weight): (60, 10), (100, 20), (120, 30). (ii) Activities (start, finish): (1,4) (3,5) (0,6) (5,7) (3,9) (5,9) (6,10) (8,11) (8,12) (2,14) (12,16).
  1. (i) Ratios6, 5, 4 → take items 1 and 2 whole (value 160, weight 30)
  2. (i) Fill the rest20 of 30 kg of item 3 → (20/30) × 120 = 80 → total 240
  3. (ii) Sort by finishpick (1,4); next start ≥ 4: (5,7); next ≥ 7: (8,11); next ≥ 11: (12,16)
(i) 240; (ii) 4 activities: (1,4), (5,7), (8,11), (12,16).
Common trap
Greedy fails on the 0/1 knapsack and on some coin systems: coins {1, 3, 4} for 6 give 4 + 1 + 1 (3 coins), but 3 + 3 uses only 2.
Practice
  1. Find: the best value.Capacity 20; items (30, 10), (20, 10), (15, 5).
    Ratios 3, 2, 3: take (30,10) and (15,5) = 45, then 5 of (20,10) = 10 → 55.
  2. Find: the Huffman cost.Frequencies 5, 9, 12, 13, 16, 45. Total bits?
    Merges 14, 25, 30, 55, 100 → total 224 bits.

2Dynamic programming: 0/1 knapsack and LCS  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Solves problems with overlapping subproblems by solving each subproblem once and storing the answer in a table.

The concept

Needs optimal substructure (best answer built from best sub-answers) and overlapping subproblems. Fill a table from small cases to large ones (tabulation) or cache a recursion (memoisation).

Where it is used

Shortest paths, sequence alignment (DNA), spell-check (edit distance), resource allocation.

0/1 knapsack
K[i][w] = max( K[i−1][w],  vi + K[i−1][w − wi] )
Skip item i, or take it (if it fits).
Longest common subsequence
L[i][j] = L[i−1][j−1] + 1 if xi = yj, else max(L[i−1][j], L[i][j−1])
Fill row by row; the answer is the bottom-right cell.
Worked example — 0/1 knapsack and LCS
(i) Capacity 5; items (weight, value): (2, 3), (3, 4), (4, 5), (5, 6). (ii) LCS of ABCBDAB and BDCABA.
  1. (i) Try the useful pairs(2,3) + (3,4) uses weight 5, value 7; any single item gives at most 6
  2. (i) The table agreesK[4][5] = 7 (take items 1 and 2)
  3. (ii) Fill the LCS table8 × 7 table; the bottom-right cell = 4
  4. (ii) Trace backone LCS is BCBA
(i) best value 7; (ii) LCS length 4 (e.g. BCBA).
Common trap
Do not use greedy by ratio on the 0/1 knapsack: items cannot be split, so the ratio order can miss the best mix.
Practice
  1. Find: the LCS length.LCS of AGGTAB and GXTXAYB.
    4 (GTAB).
  2. Answer: name the two properties.Which two properties must a problem have for DP to work?
    Optimal substructure and overlapping subproblems.

3Divide and conquer: merge sort, Karatsuba, maximum subarray  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Splits a problem into smaller independent parts, solves them recursively, and combines the answers.

The concept

Divide, conquer (recurse), combine. The cost follows from the Master theorem. Karatsuba multiplies two numbers with 3 half-size products instead of 4.

Where it is used

Sorting, fast multiplication, FFT, closest-pair, parallel computing.

Costs
merge sort: T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n = Θ(n log n);   Karatsuba: T(n) = 3T(n/2) + n = Θ(n1.585)
Karatsuba
xy = z2·102m + z1·10m + z0,   z1 = (a+b)(c+d) − z2 − z0
For x = a·10m + b, y = c·10m + d: z2 = ac, z0 = bd.
Worked example — Karatsuba and maximum subarray
(i) 12 × 34 by Karatsuba. (ii) Maximum subarray sum of [−2, 1, −3, 4, −1, 2, 1, −5, 4].
  1. (i) Splita = 1, b = 2, c = 3, d = 4
  2. (i) Three productsz2 = 1·3 = 3; z0 = 2·4 = 8; z1 = (1+2)(3+4) − 3 − 8 = 10
  3. (i) Combine3·100 + 10·10 + 8 = 408
  4. (ii) Best run4 + (−1) + 2 + 1 = 6 (elements 4 to 7)
(i) 408 with 3 multiplications; (ii) maximum subarray sum = 6.
Practice
  1. Find: the growth rate.Merge sort's recurrence T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n.
    Θ(n log n) (Master theorem, case 2).
  2. Answer: why is it faster?Why is Karatsuba faster than schoolbook multiplication?
    It uses 3 multiplications instead of 4 per split: n1.585 instead of n2.

4Backtracking: N-Queens and subset sum  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Searches all possible choices step by step, abandoning a path as soon as it cannot lead to a valid answer.

The concept

Build a partial solution, check it, extend it; if it breaks a rule, undo the last choice (backtrack) and try the next one. Pruning cuts off whole branches of the search tree. Branch and bound also prunes by a cost bound.

Where it is used

Puzzles (Sudoku, N-Queens), constraint satisfaction, combinatorial search.

Cost
worst case still exponential (e.g. 2n subsets), but pruning removes most branches in practice
Worked example — subset sum with pruning
Set {3, 5, 6, 7} (sorted), target 15. Find a subset.
  1. Take 3, 5, 6sum 14; adding 7 gives 21 > 15 → backtrack
  2. Take 3, 5, 7sum 15 ✓ found
  3. Pruning rulestop a branch as soon as the sum exceeds 15 (the numbers are sorted)
{3, 5, 7}.
Practice
  1. Find: how many solutions?How many solutions does the 4-Queens problem have?
    2.
  2. Answer: what does pruning do?What is the purpose of pruning in backtracking?
    It abandons branches that cannot lead to a solution, so far fewer paths are explored.

5Genetic algorithm  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Searches huge solution spaces for good (not always perfect) solutions by imitating evolution.

The concept

Start with a random population of encoded solutions. Repeat: score them (fitness), select parents (fitter ones more likely), combine them (crossover), make small random changes (mutation).

Where it is used

Scheduling, design optimisation, feature selection, game AI.

Selection
roulette-wheel selection: P(i) = fiΣ fj
Operators
single-point crossover: swap the tails after a cut point;   mutation: flip a bit with a small probability
Elitism copies the best solutions unchanged into the next generation.
How to solve it in the exam
  1. Encode solutions (for example as 5-bit strings) and create a random population.
  2. Compute the fitness of each.
  3. Select parents (roulette wheel or tournament).
  4. Crossover to make children, then mutate.
  5. Repeat for many generations; keep the best.
Worked example — one generation, maximise f(x) = x²
Population (5-bit): 13 = 01101, 24 = 11000, 8 = 01000, 19 = 10011.
  1. Fitness169, 576, 64, 361 → total 1170
  2. Selection probabilities0.144, 0.492, 0.055, 0.309
  3. Crossover 01101 × 11000 after bit 4children 0110|0 = 12 and 1100|1 = 25
  4. Progress252 = 625 beats the old best, 576
The fittest parent (24) is chosen most often, and crossover already produced a better child (25).
Practice
  1. Order: the steps.Arrange: crossover, fitness, mutation, selection.
    Fitness → selection → crossover → mutation.
  2. Answer: why mutate?What is mutation for?
    It keeps diversity and helps escape getting stuck in a local optimum.

6Nature-inspired algorithms: simulated annealing, PSO, ant colony  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Finds good solutions to hard optimisation problems where exact methods are too slow.

The concept

Simulated annealing sometimes accepts a worse move, with probability e−Δ/T, and lowers T over time. PSO moves particles towards their own and the swarm's best positions. Ant colony lays pheromone on good paths.

Where it is used

Routing (ACO), tuning parameters (PSO), scheduling and layout (SA).

Simulated annealing
P(accept a worse move) = e−Δ/T
High T: explore (accept often); low T: exploit (rarely accept).
Particle swarm
v ← wv + c1r1(pbest − x) + c2r2(gbest − x),   x ← x + v
Worked example — annealing acceptance
A move makes the cost worse by Δ = 10. Find the acceptance probability at T = 100 and at T = 10.
  1. T = 100e−10/100 = e−0.1 = 0.905
  2. T = 10e−10/10 = e−1 = 0.368
  3. Meaningearly on (hot) the search explores freely; later (cool) it settles down
0.905 at T = 100, 0.368 at T = 10.
Common trap
No Free Lunch: no optimiser is best on every problem. Choose the method to suit the problem.
Practice
  1. Find: the probability.Δ = 20, T = 100.
    e−0.2 ≈ 0.819.
  2. Answer: what inspired it?PSO and ACO are inspired by what?
    PSO: birds flocking / fish schooling; ACO: ants following pheromone trails.
