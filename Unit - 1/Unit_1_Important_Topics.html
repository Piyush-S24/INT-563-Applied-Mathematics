1GCD and LCM by the Euclidean algorithm  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Finds the greatest common divisor of two numbers quickly, and from it the LCM.

The concept

gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b). Keep replacing the larger number by the remainder; the last non-zero remainder is the GCD.

Where it is used

Simplifying fractions, scheduling repeating events, modular inverses and RSA.

Euclidean algorithm
gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b),    gcd(a, 0) = a
Each step keeps the same GCD but makes the numbers smaller.
LCM from GCD
lcm(a, b) = a × bgcd(a, b)
Find the GCD first, then the LCM is one division away.
How to solve it in the exam
  1. Write a = q × b + r with 0 ≤ r < b.
  2. Replace (a, b) by (b, r) and repeat.
  3. Stop when the remainder is 0. The last non-zero remainder is the GCD.
  4. LCM = product ÷ GCD.
Worked example — gcd and lcm of 252 and 105
Find gcd(252, 105) and lcm(252, 105).
  1. Divide252 = 2 × 105 + 42
  2. Divide again105 = 2 × 42 + 21
  3. And again42 = 2 × 21 + 0  →  remainder 0, stop
  4. GCDlast non-zero remainder = 21
  5. LCM252 × 10521 = 26 46021 = 1260
gcd = 21, lcm = 1260.
Common trap
The GCD is the last non-zero remainder, not the last quotient. Check: 21 divides both 252 and 105.
Practice
  1. Find: gcd and lcm.Find gcd(84, 36) and lcm(84, 36).
    84 = 2(36) + 12; 36 = 3(12) + 0, so gcd = 12. lcm = 84 × 36 / 12 = 252.
  2. Find: when do they meet again?One bus leaves every 12 minutes, another every 18 minutes. Both leave now.
    After lcm(12, 18) = 36 minutes.

2Modular arithmetic, inverses and Fermat's theorem  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Computes huge powers and divisions “mod n” quickly, and finds a number's inverse mod n.

The concept

a ≡ b (mod n) means n divides a − b. An inverse of a mod n is x with ax ≡ 1; it exists only when gcd(a, n) = 1, and the extended Euclidean algorithm finds it.

Where it is used

Cryptography (RSA), hashing, checksums, clock arithmetic.

Rules
(a + b) mod n = ((a mod n) + (b mod n)) mod n,   same for ×
Reduce at every step, so numbers never grow.
Fermat
ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p)   for prime p and gcd(a, p) = 1
Fermat's little theorem: cut the exponent down by multiples of p − 1.
Inverse
a x ≡ 1 (mod n)  ⇔  a x + n y = 1
Run Euclid, then work backwards (extended Euclid) to write 1 as a combination.
How to solve it in the exam
  1. For a power: reduce the exponent with Fermat (mod p − 1), then compute the small power.
  2. For an inverse: check gcd(a, n) = 1, run Euclid on (n, a), then substitute back to get 1 = ax + ny.
  3. Make the answer positive: add n if x is negative. Check that a × x mod n = 1.
Worked example — an inverse and a large power
(i) Find the inverse of 7 mod 26. (ii) Find 3100 mod 7.
  1. Euclid on (26, 7)26 = 3 × 7 + 5;  7 = 1 × 5 + 2;  5 = 2 × 2 + 1
  2. Work backwards1 = 5 − 2·2 = 5 − 2(7 − 5) = 3·5 − 2·7 = 3(26 − 3·7) − 2·7 = 3·26 − 11·7
  3. Read off the inverse−11 ≡ −11 + 26 = 15   (check 7 × 15 = 105 = 4·26 + 1 ✓)
  4. Fermat for (ii)7 is prime, so 36 ≡ 1 (mod 7); 100 = 6 × 16 + 4
  5. Finish3100 ≡ 34 = 81 ≡ 4 (mod 7)
7−1 ≡ 15 (mod 26); 3100 mod 7 = 4.
Common trap
An inverse does not exist if gcd(a, n) ≠ 1 (for example 4 has no inverse mod 6).
Practice
  1. Find: the remainder.Find 5117 mod 19.
    19 is prime, so 518 ≡ 1. 117 = 6 × 18 + 9, so 5117 ≡ 59. 52 = 25 ≡ 6, 54 ≡ 36 ≡ 17, 58 ≡ 289 ≡ 4, so 59 ≡ 20 ≡ 1.
  2. Find: the inverse.Find the inverse of 3 mod 11.
    3 × 4 = 12 ≡ 1 (mod 11), so the inverse is 4.

3Sets and the inclusion–exclusion principle  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Counts how many items are in at least one of several overlapping groups, without double counting.

The concept

Add the sizes of the groups, subtract what was counted twice (pairwise overlaps), add back what was removed too often (the triple overlap).

Where it is used

Counting problems, probability of “A or B”, database queries.

Two sets
|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|
Three sets
|A∪B∪C| = |A|+|B|+|C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|
Counting divisibility
Multiples of k in 1..N = ⌊N / k⌋;   multiples of both j and k = ⌊N / lcm(j, k)⌋
The overlap of “divisible by 3” and “divisible by 5” is “divisible by 15”.
Worked example — integers divisible by 3 or 5
How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 3 or by 5?
  1. Divisible by 3|A| = ⌊100/3⌋ = 33
  2. Divisible by 5|B| = ⌊100/5⌋ = 20
  3. Divisible by both (by 15)|A ∩ B| = ⌊100/15⌋ = 6
  4. Inclusion–exclusion|A ∪ B| = 33 + 20 − 6 = 47
47 integers.
Common trap
For “divisible by both” use the LCM, not the product, when the numbers share a factor (both 4 and 6 → 12, not 24).
Practice
  1. Find: how many?How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2, 3 or 5?
    50 + 33 + 20 − 16 − 10 − 6 + 3 = 74.
  2. Find: how many like neither?In a class of 40, 25 like cricket, 20 like football and 10 like both.
    Like at least one = 25 + 20 − 10 = 35, so neither = 40 − 35 = 5.

4Boolean algebra and Karnaugh maps  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Simplifies a Boolean function to the fewest terms, which means fewer logic gates.

The concept

Write the minterms (rows where F = 1) in a K-map, whose neighbouring cells differ in one variable. Group 1s in blocks of 1, 2, 4 or 8; each block drops the variables that change inside it.

Where it is used

Digital circuit design, simplifying conditions in programs.

Key laws
A + A′B = A + B,   A + AB = A,   (A + B)′ = A′B′
Absorption and De Morgan, the laws used most when simplifying by hand.
Canonical forms
F = Σm(…)  (sum of products)    |    F = ΠM(…)  (product of sums)
Minterms list the 1-rows; maxterms list the 0-rows.
How to solve it in the exam
  1. Draw the K-map with Gray-code order (00, 01, 11, 10) so neighbours differ in one bit.
  2. Put a 1 in each minterm cell.
  3. Make the largest possible groups of 1, 2, 4 or 8 adjacent 1s (edges wrap around). Every 1 must be covered.
  4. For each group, keep only the variables that stay the same inside it. Add the group terms.
Worked example — simplify F(A, B, C) = Σm(0, 2, 4, 5, 6)
Rows A B C: 0 = 000, 2 = 010, 4 = 100, 5 = 101, 6 = 110.
  1. Find a group of 4m0, m2, m4, m6 all have C = 0 (the C′ column, which wraps) → term C′
  2. Cover m5m4 and m5 are neighbours with A = 1, B = 0 → term AB′
  3. Add the groupsF = C′ + AB′
  4. Check one rowm5 = 101: C′ = 0, AB′ = 1 → F = 1 ✓; m1 = 001: both terms 0 → F = 0 ✓
F = C′ + AB′ (two terms instead of five).
Common trap
Groups must have 1, 2, 4 or 8 cells; a group of 3 or 6 is not allowed. Remember the map wraps left–right.
Practice
  1. Find: the simplified function.Simplify F(A, B, C) = Σm(1, 3, 5, 6, 7).
    m1, m3, m5, m7 have C = 1 → C; m6, m7 have A = B = 1 → AB. So F = C + AB.
  2. Answer: why Gray-code order?Why are K-map columns ordered 00, 01, 11, 10 and not 00, 01, 10, 11?
    So that neighbouring cells differ in one variable; only then can neighbours be grouped.

5Propositional logic and truth tables  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Decides when a compound statement is true, and whether two statements say the same thing.

The concept

Build a truth table with 2n rows for n variables. An implication p → q is false only when p is true and q is false. A tautology is true in every row.

Where it is used

Checking arguments, program conditions, circuit specifications.

Implication
p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q ≡ ¬q → ¬p
An implication equals its contrapositive (but not its converse q → p).
De Morgan's laws
¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q,   ¬(p ∨ q) ≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q
Worked example — is (p ∧ q) → p a tautology?
Check all four rows.
  1. p = T, q = Tp ∧ q = T, p = T → T
  2. p = T, q = Fp ∧ q = F → implication T
  3. p = F, q = Tp ∧ q = F → T
  4. p = F, q = Fp ∧ q = F → T
True in every row, so it is a tautology.
Common trap
When the “if” part is false the implication is true (vacuously). Students often write F here.
Practice
  1. Answer: when is it false?When is p → q false?
    Only when p is true and q is false.
  2. Write: the contrapositive.“If it rains, the ground is wet.”
    If the ground is not wet, it did not rain. (Same meaning as the original.)

6Mathematical induction  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Proves that a statement is true for every natural number n.

The concept

Prove it for the first value (base case). Then assume it for n = k and show it follows for n = k + 1 (inductive step). Like dominoes: the first falls, and each one knocks over the next.

Where it is used

Proving formulas for sums, divisibility, and loop invariants (that an algorithm is correct).

Principle
P(1)  and  [P(k) ⇒ P(k+1)]  ⇒  P(n) for all n ≥ 1
Strong induction assumes P(1), …, P(k) instead of just P(k).
How to solve it in the exam
  1. Base case: check the statement for n = 1 (or the first value).
  2. Hypothesis: assume it is true for n = k.
  3. Inductive step: start from the n = k + 1 case and use the hypothesis to reach the required form.
  4. Conclude: by induction it holds for all n.
Worked example — 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n2
Prove for all n ≥ 1.
  1. Base case n = 1left side = 1, right side = 12 = 1 ✓
  2. Hypothesisassume 1 + 3 + … + (2k − 1) = k2
  3. Add the next odd number1 + … + (2k − 1) + (2k + 1) = k2 + 2k + 1
  4. Factorisek2 + 2k + 1 = (k + 1)2, which is the formula for n = k + 1 ✓
True for all n ≥ 1 by induction.
Common trap
Checking a few values (n = 1, 2, 3) is not a proof. The inductive step must use the hypothesis.
Practice
  1. Prove: by induction.Show that 6 divides n3 − n for all n ≥ 1.
    Base: 1 − 1 = 0, divisible by 6. Step: (k+1)3 − (k+1) = (k3 − k) + 3k(k+1). The first part is divisible by 6 (hypothesis); k(k+1) is even, so 3k(k+1) is divisible by 6. Done.
  2. Answer: name the steps.What are the two parts every induction proof must have?
    The base case and the inductive step (assume for k, prove for k + 1).

7Relations and functions  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Classifies how elements are linked: grouping (equivalence), ordering (partial order), or mapping (function types).

The concept

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, and splits the set into classes. A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. A function is injective (one-to-one), surjective (onto) or bijective (both).

Where it is used

Grouping data (same remainder, same type), task ordering, hashing, invertible mappings.

The four properties
Reflexive: aRa;   Symmetric: aRb ⇒ bRa;   Antisymmetric: aRb ∧ bRa ⇒ a = b;   Transitive: aRb ∧ bRc ⇒ aRc
Function types
Injective: f(x1) = f(x2) ⇒ x1 = x2;   Surjective: every y has some x;   Bijective: both (so f−1 exists)
Worked example — congruence mod 3, and one function
(i) R: a R b if a ≡ b (mod 3) on {0, 1, …, 8}. (ii) f(x) = 2x + 3 on the real numbers.
  1. Reflexivea − a = 0 is divisible by 3 ✓
  2. Symmetricif 3 | (a − b) then 3 | (b − a) ✓
  3. Transitivea − c = (a − b) + (b − c), a sum of multiples of 3 ✓
  4. Classes{0, 3, 6}, {1, 4, 7}, {2, 5, 8} (same remainder)
  5. (ii) f(x) = 2x + 32x1 + 3 = 2x2 + 3 ⇒ x1 = x2 (injective); any y has x = (y − 3)/2 (surjective)
R is an equivalence relation with 3 classes; f is bijective with f−1(y) = (y − 3)/2.
Common trap
“Not symmetric” is not the same as “antisymmetric”. A relation can be neither, or even both.
Practice
  1. Answer: equivalence relation? Give the classes.R = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,2), (2,1)} on {1, 2, 3}.
    Reflexive, symmetric and transitive, so yes. Classes: {1, 2} and {3}.
  2. Answer: injective? Give the reason.f(x) = x2 on all real numbers.
    No: f(2) = f(−2) = 4. (On x ≥ 0 it would be injective.)

8Calculus: derivatives, partial derivatives and integrals  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Finds rates of change and slopes (derivatives), the direction of steepest increase (gradient), and areas or totals (integrals).

The concept

A derivative measures how fast a function changes. A partial derivative changes one variable and holds the others fixed; together they form the gradient. A definite integral adds up the area under a curve.

Where it is used

Gradient descent in machine learning, optimisation, bounding sums in algorithm analysis.

Derivatives
d/dx xn = n xn−1;   (f(g(x)))′ = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)
Power rule and chain rule.
Gradient
∇f = (∂f/∂x,  ∂f/∂y)
Points in the direction of steepest increase.
Definite integral
∫ab xn dx = [ xn+1/(n+1) ]ab
Find the antiderivative, then subtract its value at a from its value at b.
Worked example — a gradient and an integral
(i) f(x, y) = x2y + 3y2: find ∇f at (1, 2). (ii) Find ∫02 (3x2 + 2x) dx.
  1. ∂f/∂x (y fixed)2xy → at (1, 2): 2 × 1 × 2 = 4
  2. ∂f/∂y (x fixed)x2 + 6y → at (1, 2): 1 + 12 = 13
  3. Gradient∇f(1, 2) = (4, 13)
  4. Antiderivative for (ii)x3 + x2
  5. Evaluate(8 + 4) − (0 + 0) = 12
∇f(1, 2) = (4, 13); the integral = 12.
Practice
  1. Find: the derivative.d/dx (x2 + 1)3 at x = 1.
    Chain rule: 3(x2 + 1)2 × 2x = 3 × 4 × 2 = 24.
  2. Find: the gradient.f(x, y) = x2 + y2 at (3, 4).
    ∇f = (2x, 2y) = (6, 8), the direction of steepest increase.
