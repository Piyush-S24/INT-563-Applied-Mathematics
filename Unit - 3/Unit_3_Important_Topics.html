1Basic probability and the addition rule  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Finds the chance of an event, or of “A or B”, without counting overlaps twice.

The concept

With equally likely outcomes, P(A) = favourable / total. For “A or B” add the two and subtract the overlap.

Where it is used

Games, reliability, the starting point for every other probability topic.

Basics
P(A) = favourable outcomestotal outcomes,   P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
Addition rule
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
If A and B cannot happen together, the last term is 0.
Worked example — king or heart
One card is drawn from a pack of 52. Find P(king or heart).
  1. P(king)4/52
  2. P(heart)13/52
  3. Overlap: king of hearts1/52
  4. Addition rule4/52 + 13/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13
P = 4/13 ≈ 0.308.
Practice
  1. Find: the probability.Two dice are rolled. P(sum = 7)?
    6 favourable out of 36 → 1/6.
  2. Find: P(A or B).P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.2.
    0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7.

2Conditional probability, total probability and Bayes' theorem  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Updates a probability after new evidence: “given a positive test, how likely is the disease?”

The concept

P(A|B) is the chance of A once we know B happened. Total probability adds up all the ways B can happen. Bayes turns P(B|A) into P(A|B).

Where it is used

Medical tests, spam filters, fault diagnosis, Naive Bayes classifiers.

Conditional probability
P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B);   independent ⇔ P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B)
Total probability
P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|A′)P(A′)
Bayes' theorem
P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A)P(B)
How to solve it in the exam
  1. Name the events and write the given numbers: prior P(A), P(B|A), P(B|A′).
  2. Find P(B) by total probability.
  3. Apply Bayes: P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B).
  4. Interpret the answer in words.
Worked example — a disease test
1 % of people have a disease. The test is positive for 95 % of sick people and for 5 % of healthy people. A person tests positive.
  1. GivenP(D) = 0.01, P(+|D) = 0.95, P(+|D′) = 0.05
  2. Total probabilityP(+) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059
  3. BayesP(D|+) = 0.0095 / 0.059 = 0.161
  4. Meaningonly about 16 % of positives are really sick, because the disease is rare
P(disease | positive) ≈ 0.161.
Common trap
P(A|B) is not P(B|A). Here P(+|D) = 0.95 but P(D|+) is only 0.161.
Practice
  1. Find: P(A|B).P(A) = 0.3, P(B|A) = 0.8, P(B|A′) = 0.2.
    P(B) = 0.24 + 0.14 = 0.38; P(A|B) = 0.24 / 0.38 ≈ 0.632.
  2. Answer: independent? Give the reason.P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.2.
    0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 = P(A and B), so yes.

3Probability distributions: binomial, Poisson and normal  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Gives the probability of each possible count or measurement, plus its mean and variance.

The concept

Binomial: number of successes in n independent yes/no trials. Poisson: number of rare events in a fixed interval. Normal: continuous bell curve; convert to z to use the table.

Where it is used

Quality control (defects), arrivals and calls (Poisson), heights, marks and errors (normal).

Binomial
Binomial: P(X=k) = C(n,k) pk(1−p)n−k,   mean np, variance np(1−p)
Poisson
Poisson: P(X=k) = e−λ λkk!,   mean = variance = λ
Normal
Normal: z = x − μσ; about 68 % within 1σ, 95 % within 2σ, 99.7 % within 3σ
Worked example — one of each
(i) 10 fair coin tosses: P(exactly 3 heads). (ii) Calls arrive at 3 per minute: P(exactly 2 in a minute). (iii) Marks ~ N(70, 102): P(mark > 85).
  1. (i) BinomialC(10, 3)(0.5)10 = 120/1024 = 0.117; mean 5, variance 2.5
  2. (ii) Poissone−3 32/2! = 0.0498 × 4.5 = 0.224
  3. (iii) z-scorez = (85 − 70)/10 = 1.5
  4. (iii) TableP(Z > 1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668
0.117, 0.224 and 0.0668.
Practice
  1. Find: probability, mean and variance.Binomial with n = 4, p = 0.5: P(X = 2)?
    C(4, 2)/16 = 6/16 = 0.375; mean 2, variance 1.
  2. Find: the probability.X ~ N(70, 102). P(60 < X < 80)?
    That is within 1σ: ≈ 0.683.

4Frequentist inference: standard error, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Decides from a sample whether a claim about the population is believable, and gives a range for the true mean.

The concept

The sample mean varies from sample to sample by about one standard error. A 95 % confidence interval is mean ± (critical value) × SE. A test compares the sample with a claimed value H0.

Where it is used

A/B testing, quality checks, scientific studies.

Standard error and CI
SE = s√n;   95 % CI: x̄ ± z0.025·SE (z = 1.96) or t0.025, n−1·SE
Test statistic
t = x̄ − μ0s / √n;   reject H0 if |t| > critical value
Type I error: rejecting a true H0. Type II error: keeping a false H0.
How to solve it in the exam
  1. State H0 (no change) and H1, and the significance level (usually 5 %).
  2. Compute SE = s / √n and the test statistic t (or z when σ is known or n is large).
  3. Compare with the critical value from the table (df = n − 1 for t).
  4. Decide: reject H0 or fail to reject it, and say what that means.
Worked example — a one-sample t-test
A machine should fill 50 g. A sample of n = 25 gives mean 52 g, s = 5 g. Test at 5 %.
  1. HypothesesH0: μ = 50,  H1: μ ≠ 50
  2. Standard errorSE = 5 / √25 = 1
  3. Test statistict = (52 − 50)/1 = 2.0
  4. Critical valuet0.025, 24 = 2.064
  5. Decide2.0 < 2.064 → fail to reject H0
  6. Same story as a CI52 ± 2.064 × 1 = (49.94, 54.06), which contains 50
No significant evidence that the machine is off target (t = 2.0 < 2.064).
Common trap
“Fail to reject” is not “H0 is proven true”. Also: a 95 % CI does not mean a 95 % chance this particular interval holds μ.
Practice
  1. Find: SE and the 95 % CI.n = 100, mean 50, s = 20.
    SE = 20/10 = 2; CI = 50 ± 1.96 × 2 = (46.08, 53.92).
  2. Answer: which error?The test rejects H0, but H0 was actually true.
    A Type I error (false alarm).

5Bayesian inference: prior, likelihood, posterior  ★★

In one minute
What it solves

Combines what you believed before (prior) with new data (likelihood) to get an updated belief (posterior).

The concept

Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. With a Beta(a, b) prior on a probability p and k successes in n trials, the posterior is Beta(a + k, b + n − k).

Where it is used

Small-data estimation, A/B testing, spam filtering, medical decision-making.

Bayes for parameters
P(θ|data) = P(data|θ) P(θ)P(data)  ∝  likelihood × prior
Beta–Binomial
Beta(a, b) + k successes in n → Beta(a + k, b + n − k);   mean = aa + b,   MAP = a − 1a + b − 2
MLE uses the data only: k / n.
Worked example — a coin with a prior
Prior Beta(2, 2). We see 7 heads in 10 tosses.
  1. PosteriorBeta(2 + 7, 2 + 3) = Beta(9, 5)
  2. Posterior mean9 / 14 = 0.643
  3. MAP(9 − 1)/(9 + 5 − 2) = 8/12 = 0.667
  4. MLE (data only)7/10 = 0.7
  5. Readingthe prior pulls the estimate slightly towards 0.5
Posterior Beta(9, 5): mean 0.643, MAP 0.667, versus MLE 0.7.
Practice
  1. Find: the posterior and its mean.Prior Beta(1, 1); 3 heads in 4 tosses.
    Beta(4, 2); mean 4/6 = 0.667 (MLE would be 0.75).
  2. Answer: frequentist or Bayesian?Which approach treats the unknown parameter as having a probability distribution?
    Bayesian.

6Regression analysis: least squares, correlation and R²  ★★★

In one minute
What it solves

Finds the best straight line through data to predict y from x, and measures how good the fit is.

The concept

Least squares chooses the line that makes the sum of squared errors smallest. r measures the strength and direction of a linear relation (−1 to 1); R² is the share of variation in y the line explains.

Where it is used

Forecasting, trend lines, the base of machine-learning regression.

Least-squares line ŷ = a + bx
b = SxySxx = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ)Σ(x−x̄)2,   a = ȳ − b x̄
Correlation and R²
r = Sxy√Sxx Syy,   R2 = 1 − SSESST
For a simple line, R² = r².
Worked example — hours studied vs marks
x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and y = 3, 4, 6, 7, 10.
  1. Meansx̄ = 3, ȳ = 6
  2. SumsSxy = 6 + 2 + 0 + 1 + 8 = 17; Sxx = 10; Syy = 30
  3. Lineb = 17/10 = 1.7; a = 6 − 1.7 × 3 = 0.9 → ŷ = 0.9 + 1.7x
  4. FitSSE = 1.10, SST = 30 → R² = 1 − 1.1/30 = 0.963; r = 17/√300 = 0.981
  5. Predict x = 60.9 + 1.7 × 6 = 11.1
ŷ = 0.9 + 1.7x, R² = 0.963: the line explains about 96 % of the variation.
Common trap
A high correlation does not prove that x causes y.
Practice
  1. Find: the regression line.Sxy = 20, Sxx = 10, x̄ = 3, ȳ = 8.
    b = 2, a = 8 − 6 = 2 → ŷ = 2 + 2x.
  2. Answer: what does it mean?A regression has R² = 0.81.
    The line explains 81 % of the variation in y; |r| = 0.9.
